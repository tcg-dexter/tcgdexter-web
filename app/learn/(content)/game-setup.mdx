# Setting up

Every game starts the same way. It takes about a minute once you've done it twice, and getting it right matters — a botched setup is the most common way a new player accidentally cheats.

## The sequence

1. **Flip a coin.** The winner chooses who goes first.
2. **Shuffle your deck** and draw **7 cards**.
3. **Check your hand for a Basic Pokémon.** No Basic? That's a mulligan — see below.
4. **Put one Basic face down** in the Active Spot.
5. **Put up to five more Basics face down** on your Bench, if you want to. This is optional, and it's the last chance you get to set up before your opponent can interfere.
6. **Set 6 Prize cards** face down from the top of your deck.
7. **Both players turn their Pokémon face up** at the same time.
8. The player going first takes their turn.

<Callout kind="gotcha">
Steps 4 and 5 happen **face down**, before either player has seen the other's board. You're committing blind. That's deliberate — it stops both players from tailoring their opening to what the other put down.
</Callout>

## Mulligans

If your opening 7 has no Basic Pokémon, you can't set up. So:

- Reveal your hand to your opponent.
- Shuffle it back and draw a new 7.
- Repeat until you have a Basic.
- **For each mulligan you took, your opponent draws one extra card** — after both players have finished setting up.

That's the real cost. Not the lost time — the free cards you're handing over. A deck that mulligans often is a deck losing a card a game to variance before it's played anything.

<Callout kind="rule">
This is why every deck runs "enough" Basics. It's not about having something to attack with — it's about not opening with a hand you're forced to throw away.
</Callout>

## Going first

The player who goes first draws a card and takes a turn — but a restricted one.

<Callout kind="rule">
On the **first turn of the game**, the player going first:

- **cannot attack**
- **cannot play a Supporter**
- **cannot evolve** any of their Pokémon
</Callout>

What they *can* do is build a board: play as many **Basic Pokémon** to the Bench as they like, play as many **Items** as they like, attach **one Energy**, attach **Tools**, use **Abilities**, and play a **Stadium**.

The no-evolving rule isn't just a first-player restriction — **neither** player may evolve on their own first turn, and no Pokémon may evolve the turn it's played. A Pokémon has to sit through a turn before it can grow.

<Callout kind="gotcha">
A small number of cards are written specifically to break these rules, and they say so in their own text. The card always wins over the general rule — but assume the restriction applies unless you're reading otherwise.
</Callout>

So going first buys you board development and denies your opponent the first attack. It costs you your Supporter for the turn, which is a real price — going second means you can drop a big draw Supporter immediately. That trade is the whole "do you want to go first?" question, and good players answer it differently depending on the deck.

## What the board looks like when you're done

<Board />

Your deck sits face down, your six Prizes are set aside, one Pokémon is Active, and up to five are waiting on the Bench. Your discard pile is empty — for now.

<Callout kind="gotcha">
If your Active Pokémon is Knocked Out and you have nothing on the Bench to replace it, **you lose immediately**. That's why benching a second Basic during setup is almost always worth doing, even a weak one.
</Callout>
